Above the law?

Most surveyed voters want more action. What happens next?

In February 2026, 72% of surveyed US registered voters chose more investigations and prosecutions in the Epstein case. Earlier protest records offer context—but do not show how that demand translates into action.

Arvid Scarff · Berghs Growth Toolbox

In a Navigator survey conducted on 19–23 February 2026, 72% of US registered voters chose more prosecutions and investigations into people associated with Jeffrey Epstein and his criminal activities. Another 17% chose enough accountability and time to move on; 11% were unsure.[1] This measures demand for further action, not just access to documents.

Our analysis found 491 US protest event-days with Epstein-related demands in 2025, mostly about disclosure or cover-ups rather than explicit prosecution.[2] These events predate the poll. They cannot show how voters acted on the demand measured in 2026.

For wider context, consider Gaza. In September 2025, 39% of US adults surveyed by Pew Research Center said Israel was going too far in its military operation against Hamas.[3] Our analysis found 8,953 protest event-days with Gaza or Palestinian-solidarity demands across the year.[2]

The figures prompt a question, not a ranking of how much people care: how is demand for investigations and prosecutions showing up in organised protest? Answering that needs protest evidence from the same period, with demands that match the poll.

A reason to look closer

The wider comparison includes immigration, racial justice, abortion, climate and Ukraine. Epstein is not the smallest protest category: its count is above abortion and reproductive rights, and just below Ukraine solidarity. The full picture matters, not only the most striking pair.[2][4]

There are several questions worth pursuing. Do some issues have stronger groups organising protests? Does an ongoing emergency make a difference? Do people need a clear demand and someone to organise around it? What role does news coverage play?

The figures do not answer those questions. Some of the contrast may also come from comparing one case with a much broader issue, or from differences in which protests were recorded.

What the numbers mean

The Epstein poll is from February 2026; the other polls and all event counts are from 2025. The questions, populations and demands differ. We cannot say these responses caused the protests or calculate what share of supporters took part. Wanting investigations and prosecutions does not mean everyone named in the files is guilty.

An event-day means one recorded protest on one date, not a person. Repeated protests can count on several dates, and one protest can cover several issues. Of the 491 Epstein records, 425 fall on 2 August, often within broader demonstrations.[2]

That leaves a question, rather than a verdict about how much people care: what helps turn a demand for accountability into sustained public action?

Sources and calculation notes

[1] Navigator / Global Strategy Group, 19–23 February 2026. Online survey of 1,000 US registered voters, recruited through an opt-in panel and verified against a voter file. Asked which side they agreed with more, 72% chose: “There should be more prosecutions and investigations into people associated with Jeffrey Epstein and his criminal activities.” The alternatives were enough accountability/time to move on (17%) and not sure (11%). Publisher-reported margin: ±3.1 percentage points (95%); this does not cover all sources of error.

Original poll: results p. 3, methods p. 16

[2] Crowd Counting Consortium, U.S. Protest Event Data, 2025–, v18.0. Released 10 September 2026. We selected 2025 in-person records in the 50 states and DC, searched for relevant demands, and reviewed the wording to exclude ambiguous or opposing claims. Each record counts once per category. Coverage varies, differently worded demands may be missed, and scheduled repeat events may not each have been reconfirmed. This is not a complete count of all protests.

[3] Pew Research Center, 22–28 September 2025. Survey of 3,445 US adults. Asked about Israel’s current military operation against Hamas, 39% answered “Going too far.” Full-sample margin of error: ±1.9 percentage points.

Original poll, p. 8 · Report and methodology

[4] Seven selected polls. Navigator (February 2026) plus six 2025 polls from Pew, AP-NORC and Gallup. The Epstein response replaces document-release support because it measures demand for more action. Dates, questions, populations and methods differ; these snapshots are not annual averages or a shared measure of concern. This is a selected comparison, not an exhaustive review.
